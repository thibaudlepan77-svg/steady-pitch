What the song table is, and why it took so long

Every span in it was computed from transcriptions of the original recording. A song only made it in if at least two independent transcriptions of the vocal line agree within a major third. That threw out 3,426 songs and kept 324.

Sorted narrowest first, because that is the useful order for a class. 51 of them live inside eight semitones or less, which means a whole room can sing them at one pitch without anyone straining. The narrowest is Takin Care of Business, C4 to E4.

The table is in the original recorded key. Move the key and the parts that fit move with it, which is what the transposing page is for, and it is also free.

What is wrong with it

Transcriptions get octaves wrong. About one pair in five disagrees by roughly an octave, which is a machine error rather than a musical one. I drop those pairs instead of correcting them, because correcting means guessing, and a guess printed as a measurement is worse than a gap. The visible consequence is that the table leans toward songs whose melody sits in the easy middle, where transcribers agree with each other.

The kit also will not tell you someone is an alto. It gives you a first sort from range alone, which is right far more often than a guess and wrong often enough that you should listen to the five you were least sure about the following week. That sentence is in the kit too.

Who this is for

It was written for a first-year choir teacher with a class of twenty-five and one period to get through them. If you have been doing this for fifteen years you already have a routine and you do not need mine. The song table might still be worth six dollars to you, since nobody else has published one.
